AI & Water Resource Management

Independent research exploring how Artificial Intelligence can address one of the world's most pressing challenges — water scarcity.

Artificial Intelligence in Water Resource Management

Water scarcity is one of the defining challenges of our generation. In this research paper, I explore how Artificial Intelligence — from machine learning models to predictive analytics — can be applied to water resource management to help communities and governments make smarter, more sustainable decisions.

The paper examines real-world applications of AI in monitoring water quality, predicting shortages, optimising distribution networks, and responding to environmental changes driven by climate change. It argues that AI is not just a technical tool, but a critical enabler of water security for millions of people around the world.

  • Topic AI in Water Resource Management
  • Platform SSRN — Social Science Research Network
  • Status Submitted · Pending Publication
  • Focus Environmental AI · Sustainability · Machine Learning
More >>

The research began with a simple observation: despite being one of the most critical natural resources on earth, water is managed with surprisingly little data intelligence. Municipal water boards, agricultural planners, and environmental agencies often rely on outdated methods to track usage, predict demand, and respond to crises.

Artificial Intelligence changes this. Machine learning models can analyse vast datasets — satellite imagery, sensor networks, historical usage patterns — to predict where water stress will occur before it becomes a crisis. Predictive models can help farmers irrigate more efficiently, reducing waste. Anomaly detection algorithms can identify leaks in distribution networks instantly. And climate models powered by AI can help governments plan infrastructure decades in advance.

Writing this paper deepened my conviction that AI is most powerful when it is applied to challenges that affect the most people. Water is life — and AI may be one of our most powerful tools for protecting it.

Building Lawyer AI at a Law Firm

From 9th March to 17th April, I interned at a law firm where I independently designed and built an AI-powered legal tool from scratch. The project — Lawyer AI — is a tool that helps lawyers prepare and practice cases more effectively by simulating arguments, analysing case details, and providing intelligent legal insights.

This internship gave me my first real taste of building technology for a professional context. I had to understand the needs of practising lawyers, translate those needs into a working product, and iterate based on real feedback. That process taught me as much about communication and empathy as it did about code.

  • Duration 9th March – 17th April
  • Role AI Developer — Intern
  • Deliverable Lawyer AI — legal case preparation tool
  • Skills AI Development · Legal Tech · JavaScript · Product Thinking
More >>

Walking into a law firm as a student and being trusted to build something real was both exciting and daunting. Lawyers think very differently from engineers — they care about precision, precedent, and persuasion. Understanding that mindset was the first challenge, and perhaps the most important one.

I began by observing how lawyers at the firm prepared for cases — the research they did, the arguments they constructed, the ways they stress-tested their reasoning. From those observations, I designed Lawyer AI to simulate that process: feeding in case details, generating counter-arguments, and helping lawyers identify weaknesses in their own position before they faced them in court.

The internship also made me think about the broader opportunity for AI in law. Legal aid is expensive and inaccessible to millions of people. AI tools like Lawyer AI are a step toward changing that — and I hope to continue building in this space.

Bharatanatyam — A Lifelong Practice

Bharatanatyam is one of India's oldest classical dance forms, with roots stretching back over two thousand years. I have been a dedicated student of Bharatanatyam for many years, training rigorously in technique, expression, and the rich storytelling tradition of the form.

My passion for Bharatanatyam directly inspired one of my most personal projects — the Araimandi Posture Corrector, a computer vision tool built to help dancers perfect the foundational half-sit stance of the form. It was a way of bringing together two of my greatest passions: technology and dance.

More >>

Bharatanatyam demands total commitment. The practice begins with mastering the physical fundamentals — posture, footwork, hand gestures — before progressing to the emotional and narrative dimensions of performance. Every performance tells a story, drawn from mythology, devotion, and the full spectrum of human experience.

Training in Bharatanatyam has given me discipline, stage presence, and an appreciation for the value of mastery — the understanding that true skill comes only through years of patient, deliberate practice. It has also given me a deep connection to India's cultural heritage, and a desire to help preserve and share it.

The connection between my dance practice and my technology work is something I think about often. The Araimandi Posture Corrector emerged from a real problem I experienced as a dancer — the difficulty of receiving accurate, objective feedback on your posture when you cannot see yourself from the outside. Technology, in this case, became a tool for art.

Cultural Experiences

Heritage, Art & Identity

Exploring India's rich cultural and intellectual heritage — and discovering how technology can help preserve it.

Visit to the Sanskrit Academy, Bangalore

Visited the Sanskrit Academy in Bangalore to explore how ancient manuscripts are digitally scanned and preserved for future generations. Inside the manuscript room, I saw centuries-old handwritten texts being carefully digitised — some of them fragile, some faded, all of them irreplaceable. It was a powerful reminder that technology is not just about the future; it is one of our greatest tools for protecting the past.

More >>

The Sanskrit Academy, Bangalore, is one of India's most important institutions for the preservation of classical knowledge. During our visit, we learned about the painstaking process of scanning, cataloguing and digitising ancient manuscripts — some of them over a thousand years old. Each page requires careful handling, precise lighting, and expert knowledge to capture accurately.

What struck me most was the intersection of two worlds I deeply care about: technology and culture. The scholars at the Academy are using digital tools to ensure that the wisdom encoded in these ancient texts survives into the future — and that scholars, students and curious people around the world can one day access them freely.

This visit deepened my interest in how AI could accelerate this work. Machine learning models are already being used to decipher damaged manuscripts, recognise ancient scripts, and translate texts that have been inaccessible for centuries. The potential is extraordinary — and deeply exciting.
