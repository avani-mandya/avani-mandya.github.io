Building real AI technology in a professional environment — from concept to working product.

Building Lawyer AI at a Law Firm

From 9th March to 17th April, I interned at a law firm where I independently designed and built an AI-powered legal tool from scratch. The project — Lawyer AI — is a tool that helps lawyers prepare and practice cases more effectively by simulating arguments, analysing case details, and providing intelligent legal insights.

This was not a shadow internship. I was given a genuine brief — understand how lawyers work, identify a gap, and build something useful — and I delivered. The experience pushed me to think like a product designer, not just a developer, and to understand that the best technology is invisible: it solves the problem without getting in the way of the person using it.

This internship gave me my first real taste of building technology for a professional context. I had to understand the needs of practising lawyers, translate those needs into a working product, and iterate based on real feedback. That process taught me as much about communication and empathy as it did about code.

  • Duration 9th March – 17th April
  • Role AI Developer — Intern
  • Deliverable Lawyer AI — legal case preparation tool
  • Skills AI Development · Legal Tech · JavaScript · Product Thinking
More >>

Walking into a law firm as a student and being trusted to build something real was both exciting and daunting. Lawyers think very differently from engineers — they care about precision, precedent, and persuasion. Understanding that mindset was the first challenge, and perhaps the most important one.

I began by observing how lawyers at the firm prepared for cases — the research they did, the arguments they constructed, the ways they stress-tested their reasoning. From those observations, I designed Lawyer AI to simulate that process: feeding in case details, generating counter-arguments, and helping lawyers identify weaknesses in their own position before they faced them in court. The tool allows a lawyer to enter the details of a case and receive a structured breakdown of possible counter-arguments, legal risks, and suggested research directions — like having a tireless junior colleague who never misses a detail.

One of the most valuable lessons from this internship was about feedback loops. Early versions of the tool were technically correct but practically clunky — they produced output in formats that weren't suited to how lawyers actually read and reason. Iterating based on direct feedback from the lawyers at the firm taught me that the last 20% of a product — the polish, the interface, the workflow — is often what determines whether something gets used at all.

The internship also made me think about the broader opportunity for AI in law. Legal aid is expensive and inaccessible to millions of people in India and around the world. A tool that helps a single overworked lawyer serve more clients, or helps a first-generation litigant understand their own case, is a meaningful step toward changing that — and I hope to continue building in this space.

AI & Water Resource Management

Independent research exploring how Artificial Intelligence can address one of the world's most pressing challenges — water scarcity.

Artificial Intelligence in Water Resource Management

Water scarcity is one of the defining challenges of our generation. In this research paper, I explore how Artificial Intelligence — from machine learning models to predictive analytics — can be applied to water resource management to help communities and governments make smarter, more sustainable decisions.

The paper examines real-world applications of AI in monitoring water quality, predicting shortages, optimising distribution networks, and responding to environmental changes driven by climate change. It argues that AI is not just a technical tool, but a critical enabler of water security for millions of people around the world.

This was my first experience writing an independent academic research paper — and it challenged me to think rigorously, structure arguments carefully, and engage with scientific literature in a way that school projects rarely demand. Submitting it to SSRN, one of the world's leading academic networks, was a milestone that reinforced my confidence as a researcher and as a writer.

  • Topic AI in Water Resource Management
  • Platform SSRN — Social Science Research Network
  • Status Submitted · Pending Publication
  • Focus Environmental AI · Sustainability · Machine Learning
More >>

The research began with a simple observation: despite being one of the most critical natural resources on earth, water is managed with surprisingly little data intelligence. Municipal water boards, agricultural planners, and environmental agencies often rely on outdated methods to track usage, predict demand, and respond to crises. This gap between the urgency of water security and the sophistication of how it's managed is what I wanted to address.

Artificial Intelligence changes this. Machine learning models can analyse vast datasets — satellite imagery, sensor networks, historical usage patterns — to predict where water stress will occur before it becomes a crisis. Predictive models can help farmers irrigate more efficiently, reducing waste by up to 30%. Anomaly detection algorithms can identify leaks in urban distribution networks the moment they occur, saving millions of litres that would otherwise be lost. And climate models powered by AI can help governments plan water infrastructure decades in advance.

The paper also examines case studies from countries like Israel, Singapore, and parts of India where AI-driven water management has already been deployed at scale — and draws lessons for how similar approaches could be applied to regions that are most vulnerable to water scarcity, including large parts of Karnataka and the Deccan Plateau.

Choosing to submit the paper to SSRN — the Social Science Research Network — was a deliberate decision. Water management is not a purely technical problem; it is also a governance, policy, and social problem. I wanted the research to be accessible to policymakers and social scientists, not just engineers. Writing for a cross-disciplinary audience pushed me to communicate technical ideas with clarity and precision.

Writing this paper deepened my conviction that AI is most powerful when it is applied to challenges that affect the most people. Water is life — and AI may be one of our most powerful tools for protecting it.

Bharatanatyam — A Lifelong Practice

Bharatanatyam is one of India's oldest classical dance forms, with roots stretching back over two thousand years. I have been a dedicated student of Bharatanatyam for many years, training rigorously in technique, expression, and the rich storytelling tradition of the form. Every class is a practice in patience — the forms are exacting, the footwork demanding, and mastery comes only through years of repetition and refinement.

Bharatanatyam has taught me more than dance. It has taught me the meaning of discipline — the kind that keeps you returning to the practice even when progress feels slow. It has given me stage presence, the ability to be fully present in the moment, and a deep respect for art forms that carry centuries of cultural wisdom within them.

My passion for Bharatanatyam directly inspired one of my most personal projects — the Araimandi Posture Corrector, a computer vision tool built to help dancers perfect the foundational half-sit stance of the form. It was a way of bringing together two of my greatest passions: technology and dance.

More >>

Bharatanatyam demands total commitment. The practice begins with mastering the physical fundamentals — posture, footwork, hand gestures — before progressing to the emotional and narrative dimensions of performance. Every performance tells a story, drawn from mythology, devotion, and the full spectrum of human experience.

Training in Bharatanatyam has given me discipline, stage presence, and an appreciation for the value of mastery — the understanding that true skill comes only through years of patient, deliberate practice. It has also given me a deep connection to India's cultural heritage, and a desire to help preserve and share it.

The connection between my dance practice and my technology work is something I think about often. The Araimandi Posture Corrector emerged from a real problem I experienced as a dancer — the difficulty of receiving accurate, objective feedback on your posture when you cannot see yourself from the outside. Technology, in this case, became a tool for art.

Cultural Experiences

Heritage, Art & Identity

Exploring India's rich cultural and intellectual heritage — and discovering how technology can help preserve it.

Visit to the Sanskrit Academy, Melkote

Visited the Sanskrit Academy in Melkote, Karnataka to explore how ancient manuscripts are digitally scanned and preserved for future generations. Inside the manuscript room, I saw centuries-old handwritten texts being carefully digitised — some of them fragile, some faded, all of them irreplaceable. It was a powerful reminder that technology is not just about the future; it is one of our greatest tools for protecting the past.

The Academy houses thousands of manuscripts spanning philosophy, mathematics, astronomy, poetry, and medicine — written in Sanskrit, Kannada, Tamil, and other classical languages. Seeing this wealth of knowledge, and understanding how vulnerable it is to the passage of time, gave me a new appreciation for the urgency of digital preservation.

More >>

The Sanskrit Academy, Melkote, is one of India's most important institutions for the preservation of classical knowledge. During our visit, we learned about the painstaking process of scanning, cataloguing and digitising ancient manuscripts — some of them over a thousand years old. Each page requires careful handling, precise lighting, and expert knowledge to capture accurately.

What struck me most was the intersection of two worlds I deeply care about: technology and culture. The scholars at the Academy are using digital tools to ensure that the wisdom encoded in these ancient texts survives into the future — and that scholars, students and curious people around the world can one day access them freely.

This visit deepened my interest in how AI could accelerate this work. Machine learning models are already being used to decipher damaged manuscripts, recognise ancient scripts, and translate texts that have been inaccessible for centuries. The potential is extraordinary — and deeply exciting.
